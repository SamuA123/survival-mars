# Teacher guide: Survival Mars

**Survival Mars** is a free browser game (no downloads or accounts) in which students run a base on the NE Syrtis plateau, at the edge
of the Isidis basin on Mars. It fits a **45 to 60 minute** class in science, technology or systems thinking.

Link: https://survival-mars.pages.dev · Ages 7 to 12 · Works on computers, tablets and phones · Available in English and Spanish

---

## 1. What do students learn?

| Key idea | How they experience it in the game |
|---|---|
| **Resources are limited and everything has a cost** (trade-offs) | There is not enough power for everything: turning on the lab leaves less for oxygen or food. |
| **Connected systems** | A dust storm cuts solar power, so if the battery runs out there is a blackout, systems shut down and oxygen drops. |
| **Plan before you run out** | Earth announces the storm 2 sols ahead: whoever charges the battery survives. |
| **Radiation and protection** | Mars has almost no atmosphere and no magnetic field. Sheltering during a flare and covering the habitat with regolith lowers the dose. |
| **Risk vs. reward** | Spacewalks bring science and oxygen, but every second outside uses suit air and adds radiation. |
| **Building protection takes work** | Regolith is collected outside (4-item backpack), deposited at the base and spent in build mode (B key): each shielding section lowers the radiation that gets into the habitat. |
| **Looking after every person** | Each astronaut (Lía, Kofi, Mei, Teo) has a dosimeter. A spacewalk only irradiates whoever goes out: sharing the walks keeps everyone further from the limit. |
| **The Sun changes the risk** | An indicator shows solar activity (NASA DONKI). If a strong flare appears, an alert arrives: keep working or go back to the shelter? |
| **Light has a speed** | A message to Earth takes minutes: in an emergency, the crew decides on its own. |
| **Science uses real data** | The flares come from NASA's DONKI database, and every consequence cites a real study. |

**Vocabulary that appears (and is explained in the game):** sol (Martian day), regolith, radiation, mSv (millisievert),
solar flare, oxygen, solar power, battery, solar conjunction, spacewalk (EVA), dosimeter, shielding.

## 2. Before you start
- Open the link on the projector once so it loads (it needs internet).
- **Choose the rank** on the start screen:
  - **Cadet (ages 7 to 9):** 10 sols, 15 seconds to decide, no communication blackout.
  - **Commander (ages 10 to 12):** 15 sols, 10 seconds to decide, with a solar conjunction.
- The **voice narrator** reads the texts aloud: useful for early readers or students with reading difficulties.
- The game can be played **in pairs or groups of three** (one drives, the others decide): it sparks more discussion.
- The tutorial appears the first time; it can be repeated with the help (?) button.
- **Controls:** WASD or arrow keys to walk (joystick on tablets and phones) · **E** or **Q** pick up · **R** drop · **E** at the door to deposit and go in · **B** build shielding · **Esc** close.
- **Every game is different:** the dust storm, solar flares and other emergencies fall on different sols each time, so two groups will not see exactly the same mission.
- **To show the solar alert to the whole class**, open the link with `?alerta=1`: a **drill** appears (the screen says so). Real alerts only appear if NASA records a strong flare during the class.

## 3. Lesson plan (45 to 60 min)

### Opening (10 min): starter question
> "If you lived on Mars and only had power for **two** of these things, which would you choose?
> breathing · growing food · talking to Earth · doing science"

Write the answers on the board. There is no right one: the point is to discover that **they all matter** and must be balanced.

### Main activity (25 to 35 min): play
- A first free game (losing is normal: there is even a badge for "Learning from mistakes").
- Ask students to fill in the **Commander's log** (section 5) while they play.
- Pause the whole class when someone gets the **dust storm warning** or a **solar flare**: "What did you decide and why?"
- Before the second spacewalk, ask: "Who in the crew should go out this time? Look at the dosimeters."

### Closing (10 to 15 min): discussion
Use the **Mission report** on the final screen (timeline, charts and badges) to compare strategies between groups.

## 4. Discussion questions

**Power and trade-offs**
1. Which system did you turn off first when power ran short? Why that one?
2. If the battery runs out during the storm, what shuts down by itself? Is that the best choice?
3. Why does a Mars base need a reactor as well as solar panels? (Hint: storms and nights.)

**Radiation**

4. Why is there more radiation on Mars than on Earth? (Thin atmosphere, no magnetic field.)
5. During a flare, what do you lose if you take shelter? And if you don't?
6. Why does covering the house with soil (regolith) protect you?
7. Which shielding section did you build first? How much did the radiation per sol drop?
8. Did you always send the same astronaut outside? What happened to their dosimeter compared with the others?

**Risk and exploration**

9. On the spacewalk, did you go far for the most valuable rock? Did you come back with little oxygen? Was it worth it?
10. With a full backpack, did you go back to deposit or drop something to carry something else? Why?
11. What happens if the suit runs out of oxygen? Why does the game rescue you instead of letting you "die"?

**Communication**

12. You asked Earth for help and the answer arrived the next sol. Why does it take so long?
13. How would you feel without being able to talk to anyone on Earth for 2 weeks (solar conjunction)?

**Real science**

14. Open the NASA data panel. What is real data, what is stored, and what is a game rule? Which fact surprised you most?

## 6. Activities to keep learning
- **Today's Sun:** check in the game which real flares NASA recorded in the last 30 days (start screen or the NASA data panel).
  Would today be a good day for a spacewalk?
- **Maths:** if Mars gives ~0.7 mSv per day and Earth ~2.4 mSv per year, how many days on Mars equal one year on Earth? (about 3.5 days)
- **Speed of light:** if a message takes 12 minutes to arrive, how long do you wait for an answer? (24 minutes)
- **Space garden:** grow lettuce or radishes in the classroom under different amounts of light, like the VEG-05 experiment on the Space Station.
- **Design your base:** draw a Martian base and explain which system you would set up first and why.

## 7. Frequently asked questions
- **Does it need an account or installation?** No. Just a modern browser and internet.
- **Does it store student data?** Only in the browser itself (preferences, tutorial seen, badges and the saved mission to continue later). There is no sign-up and no user server.
- **Are the data real?** The flares come from a recent query of NASA DONKI (or from real historical flares if there is no connection, and the game says so).
  The health findings come from NASA OSDR studies. Some power and food numbers are simplified so they are easy to understand;
  see [`DATOS_NASA.md`](DATOS_NASA.md).
- **How long is a game?** About 10 minutes (Cadet) to 15 minutes (Commander).
